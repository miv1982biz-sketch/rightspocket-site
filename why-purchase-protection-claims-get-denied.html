Why purchase protection claims get denied

When a purchase-protection or extended-warranty claim is denied, it is rarely because the benefit didn't apply. It is usually one of five fixable problems: the wrong card, a missed clock, a missing document, an excluded item, or a claim filed with the wrong party. Here they are, in the order they tend to bite.

1. The purchase wasn't on that card

These benefits attach to the card that paid. Pay with a different card, split the purchase across two cards, check out with a stored card you forgot you had, or let a partner's card take the charge — and the claim has no home. Before you file, find the actual charge on the actual statement. That statement is also one of the documents the claim will ask for, which brings us to the pattern behind this whole list: the paperwork is the product.

2. The filing window closed

Coverage windows get the attention (often 90–120 days for purchase protection), but claims also carry a shorter duty: open the claim promptly after the damage, theft or breakdown — sometimes within a set number of days of the incident. People lose valid claims by waiting until the weekend, then the next one. If something breaks or is stolen, the claim is a same-week errand. For theft, many benefits also require a police report filed within a stated period — another clock, and another document.

3. A document is missing

The typical packet: itemized receipt, card statement showing the purchase, photos of the damage, and — for extended warranty — a copy of the manufacturer's warranty; sometimes a repair estimate or a repair shop's statement that the item can't be fixed. A missing piece usually doesn't kill a claim outright; it stalls it, and stalls run into clocks (see reason 2). The fix is boring and total: keep the receipt and the statement, photograph the damage before you clean anything up, and save the warranty document the day you buy the product, not the day it breaks.

4. The item is in an excluded category

Every Guide to Benefits carries an exclusion list, and the usual suspects recur: motorized vehicles and their parts, consumables, perishables, items bought for resale or commercial use, and — a classic trip-up — items bought used or second-hand under some cards' purchase protection. Jewelry, cash equivalents and gift cards have their own special limits on many cards. None of this is hidden; it's in the guide, which is why reading your own card's guide beats any general article, including this one.

5. The claim went to the wrong door

Card benefits are administered by a benefits administrator, not by the bank's general support line and not by the store. Filing with the retailer, or disputing the charge instead of claiming the benefit, sends you down a corridor that doesn't lead to the benefit. Your Guide to Benefits names the administrator and the claim channel (usually a dedicated phone line and claims portal). Start there.

The short version

Right card, opened on time, complete documents, eligible item, correct door. Five checks, and most denials never happen. If you haven't yet, read how purchase protection and extended warranty work — including how to find your own card's Guide to Benefits in about five minutes, free.
